## Why this session

The team director asked for two things: the risks and opportunities, and practical use of the Curtin AI tools that fit the team's work. The team's real gain is not in any single prompt. It is in knowing where Copilot can see, moving work between apps on purpose, and checking the result before it leaves the building.

The session uses the team's own material: client proposals, tender responses, marketing organisation and client/BD management. Everyone leaves with two or three tasks from their own week, not a generic demo.

## The one idea

You are the orchestrator.

Curtin's Copilot keeps work data inside Curtin's tenant. It does not pass work between apps for you. Copilot in Outlook can see the email you have open. Copilot Chat in the browser cannot, unless you paste the text in or upload the file. Copilot in Word knows the document, not the thread that led to it.

So the skill is not "automate the chain". The skill is: get the right context in front of the right Copilot, then carry the output to the next step yourself.

Everything in the morning hangs off that idea.

## Learning outcomes

By 12:30, each participant can:

1. Say what their own Copilot can and cannot see, and why the answer depends on the app and their licence.
2. Move a piece of work between Outlook, Word, Excel and Copilot Chat without losing control of it.
3. Write an RTCF prompt (role, task, context, format) and add an upgrade when the output misses.
4. Take a client brief to a draft proposal outline in Word, then get a useful critique of it.
5. Name the Curtin rules on what may go into an AI tool, and the human checkpoints that stay with the person.
6. Leave with two or three named tasks to try in the following week.

## 9:00 to 9:15 Welcome and context pre-flight

Open with the problem, not the product. Ask the room: when you open Copilot Chat and ask about an email, what happens? Most people will have found that it cannot see the mailbox, or that it can only see one open message, or that a colleague's screen behaves differently.

That gap is the session.

Then run the pre-flight, five minutes, everyone at once:

1. Open Copilot Chat at copilot.cloud.microsoft. Note the label under your name.
2. Ask: "What can you see about my work right now?"
3. Open Outlook, select an email, open the Copilot pane, and ask the same question.
4. Open Word on a sample document and ask again.

Capture the differences on the whiteboard. The room will produce three or four different answers depending on licence and app. That is the lesson, and it is more convincing than a slide.

Instructor note: Microsoft is changing this behaviour through late 2026. A roadmap item (RM554934) extends Copilot Chat in Outlook to reason over the whole inbox and calendar for people without an add-on licence, due around October 2026. Do not teach a fixed answer. Teach people to check their own setup and to re-check after an update. The pre-flight is the reusable skill.

## 9:15 to 10:00 Session 1: opportunity and risk

### Two guiding questions (2 minutes)

Write these on the whiteboard and leave them there all morning:

1. How can we automate what we already do?
2. What could we never do until now?

They keep the session on opportunity as well as risk. Come back to them at the close.

### Where it genuinely helps in Exec Ed work (10 minutes)

Keep this concrete and tied to the team's week:

- A first draft of anything: a proposal section, a program description, a follow-up email, a marketing blurb.
- Turning a long brief or transcript into a one-page summary, or a summary into a slide outline.
- Restructuring text for a different reader: academic to client, client to internal.
- A critique pass before a document goes to the director or to a client.
- Excel: explaining a formula, suggesting a cleaner structure, drafting a budget table from a list.
- Meeting follow-up: turning notes into actions, owners and dates.

The pattern: Copilot is strong at first drafts, restructuring, summarising and critique. It is weak at knowing your client, your numbers and your commitments.

### Where it trips people up (10 minutes)

- It agrees with you. Push back and it folds. That is sycophancy, not agreement.
- It invents specifics: names, dates, figures, citations, policy clauses. Fluency is not evidence.
- It uses stale context. A document from last year, an old brief, an out-of-date fee schedule.
- It loses the thread between apps. The outline in Word does not know what you agreed in Outlook.
- It writes confidently about things it cannot see.

Live demo, five minutes: ask Copilot to justify a weak proposal. Then argue the opposite and watch it switch sides. Then ask it to list what it does not know. This lands better than any warning slide.

### The trade-offs (5 minutes)

AI does not remove trade-offs. It moves them. Name the four that matter for this team:

| Tension | If you lean too far | What to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking vs replacing | You stop forming your own view | Use it to support reasoning, not bypass it |
| Speed vs safety | Data leaks, thin work goes out | Start with safe material and the approved tool |
| One-shot vs conversation | Superficial, brittle output | Most good work needs a second and third turn |
| Tool vs workflow | Shiny object, no lasting gain | The workflow is the win, not the tool |

### The rules, in plain language (10 minutes)

Not legal advice. The team's operating rules, aligned to Curtin policy.

### Which tools (2 minutes)

- Microsoft Copilot is Curtin's official tool for work data.
- Claude is available through an enterprise agreement by application. If someone has access, it is governed, but check with IT before client or participant data goes in.
- ChatGPT, Gemini and personal Copilot are for personal exploration with mock or public material only.

If you are not sure whether a tool is approved, treat it as not approved.

### What may go in (8 minutes)

- Only put into Copilot what you would put into a Curtin system. Enterprise Copilot keeps prompts and responses inside the tenant, but that does not make any content appropriate to enter.
- Client and participant personal information, pricing, margins and contract terms need a reason to be there. Prefer the sanitised brief. If in doubt, ask before you paste.
- Do not paste third-party material that the client has not cleared for this use, or material covered by a confidentiality clause you have not checked.
- Treat every output as a draft by an unknown author. A person signs off. The person who sends it owns it.
- Check facts, names, figures and commitments. Especially figures.
- Record keeping: if the output becomes a Curtin record, it follows the same record-keeping rules as any other document.
- Personalisation and memory: Copilot remembers context to be useful. Know how to review and clear it. Cover this in the settings guide.

Close the block with the accountability line: Copilot can draft, but it cannot be accountable. That stays with the person whose name is on the email.

## 10:00 to 10:30 Session 2: context, and carrying work between apps

### What Copilot can see (8 minutes)

Three variables decide what Copilot knows:

1. **The app.** Copilot in Outlook sees the selected email and, with a licence, the mailbox. Copilot in Word sees the open document. Copilot Chat in Edge sees the page and whatever you paste or upload.
2. **The licence.** Copilot Chat (Basic) is mostly web plus what you upload. A Copilot add-on licence adds work data through Work IQ and Microsoft Graph: mail, calendar, files, chats, meetings.
3. **The tenant configuration.** Curtin's admins decide which features are on. Two people on the same licence can still see different buttons.

Draw the simple diagram on the whiteboard: four boxes (Outlook, Word, Excel, Chat) with a wall between each. The wall is the point. You are the doorway.

### The carry pattern (10 minutes)

Demonstrate one loop, live, end to end:

1. In Outlook, select a client email and ask Copilot for the three decisions the client is asking for. Copy the answer.
2. In Word, on a blank page, paste it and ask for a proposal outline in the house structure.
3. In Copilot Chat, paste the outline and ask for the weakest section and what a sceptical client would challenge.
4. Back in Word, revise the outline yourself.
5. In Excel, turn the scope list into a simple budget table.

Say the rule out loud: every arrow in that loop is a copy and paste by a person. That is not a limitation to apologise for. It is where judgement enters.

### Edge and the browser (4 minutes)

- Sign in with the Curtin account. Work and web are different modes.
- Uploading a file is a deliberate act. Check you have the right file and the right permissions.
- The page you are on is context. Be careful what is open on screen when you share or demo.

### RTCF and four upgrades (6 minutes)

RTCF is the brief. Cover it here, then use it in Session 3.

- **Role:** who should write this. "You are an experienced proposal writer who writes plainly."
- **Task:** the verb and the scope. "Draft a one-page proposal outline."
- **Context:** the reader, the situation, the standard, and the source pasted in.
- **Format:** length, structure, tone.

Context does most of the work. Paste the source rather than describing it.

Four upgrades, for when the output still misses:

1. **Example.** Paste one you liked and ask it to match the shape, not the content.
2. **Done.** Define success, and mark anything uncertain with [CHECK] rather than guessing.
3. **Questions.** Ask it to put up to five questions before it starts.
4. **Steps.** Outline first, then draft one section at a time.

### When carrying is not practical (2 minutes)

Show the Steps upgrade as a single-prompt workflow: summarise the brief, identify the decisions, draft an outline, list the gaps, one step at a time. Say clearly that this does not join the apps. It just stops five separate prompts in one chat.

## 11:00 to 11:45 Session 3: brief to proposal outline (hands-on)

Work in pairs or threes. Use the sanitised samples in `source-documents/samples/`. One is a professional development proposal brief, one is an international tender response. Both keep the structure of the real documents with client names, staff names, rates and totals removed.

Name the arc before they start: find (Outlook), draft (Word), challenge (Chat or Word), quantify (Excel), send (Outlook). The four exercises are one chain, not four separate tasks. Each one picks up what the last produced.

### Demo: the broken formula (3 minutes)

Open Excel with a worked example and a deliberately broken formula. Ask Copilot: "What is wrong with this formula, and how do I fix it?" Then ask it to explain the fix in plain English.

This is the fastest win in the room. Most people have a spreadsheet that has cost them an afternoon. Show the fix, then ask who has one they would like to bring to Exercise 4.

### Exercise 1: the brief (8 minutes)

Read the sample brief. In Copilot Chat (or Word), ask for:

- the client's stated outcomes
- what they actually need but did not say
- the three decisions the proposal must help them make
- the questions we should ask before writing

Compare answers across pairs. The differences show how much the prompt and the pasted context matter.

### Exercise 2: the outline (15 minutes)

Move to Word. Paste the brief and the Exercise 1 output. Ask for a proposal outline that follows the house structure: context, outcomes, modules, delivery, fees, why Curtin.

Then tighten it with a second prompt: "Cut anything that is generic. Keep only what is specific to this brief. Mark anything you inferred rather than read."

### Exercise 3: the critique (14 minutes)

Ask Copilot to red-team the outline as a sceptical client:

- What would make you doubt this provider?
- Which claims are unsupported?
- Where does the outline answer a question the client did not ask?
- What is missing from the evaluation criteria?

Then ask for a revision plan, not a rewrite. Participants revise one section by hand. The point is that the critique is useful and the rewrite is theirs.

### Exercise 4: the budget table (5 minutes, if time)

In Excel, paste the scope list and ask for a table with columns for activity, assumption, quantity and rate. Leave rates blank for the team to fill. Ask Copilot to explain any formula it writes.

## 11:45 to 12:15 Session 4: make it yours

This is the block the director flagged as the real benefit: organisation of marketing, and management of client/BD.

### Map one workflow (8 minutes)

Each person picks one recurring task and writes it as five steps: input, what they do now, where it hurts, where Copilot could draft, where a person must check.

Common candidates from this team:

- New client enquiry to a first proposal draft.
- Post-program follow-up: feedback, summary, next-cohort pitch.
- Marketing calendar: turning a program description into a six-week campaign outline.
- Client/BD pipeline: turning meeting notes into a CRM-style update and a follow-up email.
- Tender response: mapping evaluation criteria to existing evidence.

### Build the reusable prompt (9 minutes)

Turn the chosen task into an RTCF template with named slots: role, task, context, format, plus a check and a never line. Save it somewhere the team can find it. The prompt library in `materials/prompt-library.md` has starters.

### Put the checkpoints in (4 minutes)

For the chosen workflow, mark where a person must check before the work moves on: before anything goes to a client, before any figure is quoted, before any commitment is made, before anything is filed as a record.

This is the answer to "where does AI fit and will it work every week". It works when the checkpoints are named and the handoffs are deliberate.

### Agents, briefly (5 minutes)

An agent is a brief that remembers. Three tiers:

1. Built-in: Analyst, Researcher, Facilitator, Writing Coach, Idea Coach. You use them, you do not build them.
2. Custom: instructions plus files, built inside Copilot. This is the Custom GPT equivalent. It can ask questions, take a file and draft, but it does not talk to other agents or run on its own.
3. Copilot Studio: actions and systems. A project with an owner, a knowledge source and governance. Out of scope today.

If someone wants to build one, map it to their RTCF prompt: the instructions are the brief, the knowledge is the file, the conversation is the iteration. Sharing is blocked on the tenant, so it stays personal. The rule: do not automate a step you have not done manually twice.

### Five questions (4 minutes)

Each person answers these on paper or in the workbook:

1. What is the task or challenge I am exploring?
2. What is my first RTCF prompt?
3. What did I like or not like about the response?
4. What follow-up prompt would improve it?
5. How might this become part of my routine?

## Questions that will come up

**Is this an agent?** Only if it has its own instructions and knowledge and you open it by name. What most teams build is a saved prompt with a name and some files. It does not talk to other agents or run on its own. Microsoft ships several agents you can just use: Analyst, Researcher, Facilitator, Writing Coach, Idea Coach.

**Can I use Claude?** It is available through an enterprise agreement by application. If you have access, it is governed, but check with IT before client or participant data goes in. Copilot remains the official default.

**Can I connect Chat to my inbox?** Not by switching a setting. Use the Copilot pane inside Outlook, or paste the email into Chat. Microsoft is extending this through late 2026, so check your own screens.

**Why does it agree with me?** It is optimised to be helpful, not to be right. Use the guardrail prompt: only change the assessment if a new fact arrives, and name the fact that changed it.

**Is the output confidential?** The tenant protects data in transit and at rest. It does not make the output correct or approved. A person still signs off.

**What about participant data?** Use the approved tool, the minimum data, and the policy. If in doubt, use a sanitised version and ask.

**Will this replace my job?** No. It removes keyboard time. The judgement, the client relationship and the accountability stay with you.

## Why the day is shaped this way

The sequence follows ADKAR, the change model, without naming it to the room:

- **Awareness:** Session 1, the risks and the trade-offs.
- **Desire:** their own task, the demos, the broken-formula win.
- **Knowledge:** context, the carry pattern, RTCF.
- **Ability:** Session 3, one workflow end to end.
- **Reinforcement:** the commit, the checklist, the four-week check-in, and one or two champions.

The four-week check-in is the Reinforcement step. Ask the director to nominate one or two champions who can share wins and answer questions between sessions.
